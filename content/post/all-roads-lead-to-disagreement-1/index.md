> “About these matters there is no scientific basis on which to form any calculable probability whatever. We simply do not know.”
>
> — John Maynard Keynes, “The General Theory of Employment” (1937), p. 214.

Modern finance is technically magnificent and epistemically credulous. Its sophistication operates downstream of judgments that seldom receive the scrutiny lavished on the calculations they support: which futures deserve to be represented, which observations are informative about them, and how much authority current prices should have in describing a world that has not yet arrived. Once those matters have been settled, calibration, hedging and optimisation can proceed with extraordinary precision. The difficulty is that precision in the calculation does not establish the soundness of the assumptions that made it possible.

I came to this problem through valuation rather than through a prior interest in disagreement. Long-dated assets still have to be valued when historical calibration is weak, the relevant policy regime is contested, and the market price reflects the constraints of its participants as well as their expectations. Constructing a serious alternative valuation required more than changing a parameter. It required an account of what justified the probability picture in the first place, what remained open after the available evidence had been used, and what financial consequences could reasonably follow from preferring one picture to another. The distinction between recognising a discrepancy and being able to profit from it eventually proved as important as the distinction between a forecast and a price.

## The conditions behind the calculation

Conventional probabilistic finance is strongest where three conditions hold together: the relevant outcomes are stable enough to describe, the historical record is informative enough to discipline the model, and disagreement is bounded enough for prices to coordinate decisions without disclosing a single agreed account of the future. Much of ordinary treasury practice and short-horizon risk management can operate within such a setting. Nobody needs to suppose that every parameter is known or that a liquidity crisis is impossible. What matters is that errors can usually be discussed within a sufficiently shared description of the problem.

The distinction between risk and Knightian uncertainty concerns how much is known about probabilities within such a description. Radical uncertainty reaches further: it concerns the adequacy of the description itself. A model can accommodate uncertainty about the rate at which an existing technology improves while omitting the possibility that a different technology makes it obsolete. Adding a distribution over the first rate does not admit the second possibility. Enlarging the model may help, but deciding how to enlarge it is already part of the problem, rather than a calculation the old model can perform on its own. Knight's distinction and Keynes's discussion of investment under uncertainty remain useful precisely because they prevent these different difficulties from being concealed inside one more volatility parameter.[1]

Climate transition brings them together unusually clearly. Physical and engineering knowledge constrain what can happen, while policy, investment, technological substitution and financing determine how those constraints reach firms and households. The record contains evidence about each mechanism without containing repeated instances of the whole transformation. Scenarios make the problem tractable by supplying conditional worlds that can be examined and revised; they do not exhaust the possible future, and they do not acquire probabilities merely by being placed side by side. A family drawn too narrowly excludes the possibilities that matter, whereas one drawn without sufficient restriction can justify almost any decision. Choosing its scope is consequential work.

An objection is often raised at this point in the name of empirical discipline. George Frankfurter recounts an editor of a leading finance journal telling him: “We in finance,” he remarked, “do not care much about philosophy. We care about data and what data can tell us.”[2] The useful question is what, in the problem at hand, the data can tell us. Historical defaults can constrain a credit mechanism, engineering evidence can rule out a proposed production path, and observed financing behaviour can undermine an assumption about investment. None of those tests becomes less valuable because the full transition has no close precedent. But neither does passing them supply the repeated experiment needed to validate every long-horizon implication of the assembled model.

Backtesting therefore retains a substantial jurisdiction without retaining sovereignty. A model should face the observations capable of contradicting it, alongside accounting identities, physical constraints, rival specifications and sensitivity analysis. Internal consistency is necessary, although an internally consistent story may still be false; robustness is informative, although robustness across a poorly chosen family can be reassuring for the wrong reason. What has to change is the correspondence between the claim and the test. Requiring a non-repeatable transformation to supply the same evidence as a recurring short-horizon process is an odd way of demonstrating respect for observation.

## Before the prior, after the posterior

Bayesian reasoning offers a natural discipline here, provided its scope is not reduced to the application of Bayes' rule. In the narrower exercise, the hypotheses, prior and likelihood have already been specified. Conditioning then tells us how the probabilities should change when evidence arrives. That operation can be performed faultlessly while the model excludes a material possibility or assigns unwarranted authority to a particular source. A posterior is an answer conditional on the structure that produced it, not an independent assessment of that structure.[3]

A broader Bayesian practice begins before the prior and continues after the posterior. It asks how the question should be formulated, which scientific and institutional knowledge belongs in it, which model families merit comparison, and what observations would require their revision. It also asks what to do when the inquiry leaves several probability pictures standing but the institution must nevertheless approve a project, set a budget or hold a position. Retaining the family and choosing a decision that performs acceptably across it is one response; adopting a declared aggregation or selection rule is another. Neither follows merely from having calculated a posterior, and there is no reason to pretend that every decision requires the same response.

Maximum entropy belongs to this broader undertaking. When the available information constrains a probability distribution without specifying it completely, the principle supplies a rule for completing the description without introducing additional structure beyond that licensed by the chosen criterion. On a specified finite set of outcomes, with equal reference weights, this means maximising Shannon entropy subject to the retained constraints. With a non-uniform reference, the corresponding minimum-relative-entropy construction selects a compatible distribution that departs least from that reference in the specified sense. These procedures can help construct a prior, but their role is not confined to the beginning of an analysis: they also provide a declared completion when partial information must be turned into one operative probability picture.[3]

The qualification is part of the method. Outcomes must have been distinguished, a reference structure supplied, constraints justified and the treatment of dependence stated before “least committal” has a determinate meaning. Existence and uniqueness also require their own conditions. The resulting distribution is not thereby the true law, nor is minimum relative entropy automatically the right objective for an investor. Its merit is that a particular act of completion can be justified and inspected, instead of occurring silently through a convenient distributional assumption or an unexplained average of scenarios.

There is an organisational consequence as well. An institution may avoid a Dutch book, a combination of accepted bets that guarantees a loss, and still use one transition assumption in disclosure, another in lending, and a third in risk management without explaining their relationship. Different purposes can warrant different assessments; unexplained differences should not be mistaken for that justification. Likewise, revising a model after new evidence is essential, whereas altering the standard of revision to accommodate an embarrassing result is something else. Broad Bayesian discipline reaches into these choices, but it cannot furnish all their premises from nothing. It is at this boundary, rather than at the limits of numerical sophistication, that the representational residue appears.

## What remains undecided

By *representational residue* I mean the part of the inferential problem that must be fixed to obtain a determinate answer, but that logic, the available evidence and further calculation within a candidate representation do not uniquely select. It can concern the state space, the language of relevant features, the model family, the reference measure or the treatment of dependence. The definition does not include every judgment an analyst makes. Where evidence or a compelling symmetry settles a choice, there is no residue on that point. Nor does it require agreement with any particular philosophy of probability: the issue is what has been supplied before the inferential machinery can begin to choose.

Bertrand's chord problem is a useful illustration. Ask for the probability that a random chord of a circle is longer than the side of its inscribed equilateral triangle, without specifying how it is drawn. Choosing its midpoint uniformly over the disc gives one quarter; choosing two endpoints independently and uniformly on the circumference gives one third; choosing its distance from the centre uniformly gives one half. These are different sampling laws, not contradictory answers obtained from the same law. A change of coordinates, with the probability measure correctly transported, leaves the answer unchanged. The ambiguity comes from treating uniformity in each new description as though it were the same expression of ignorance.[4]

The financial counterpart is less picturesque and more consequential. For a perpetual stream with next-period cash flow \(C\), discount rate \(r\) and growth rate \(g<r\), the familiar valuation is \(V=C/(r-g)\). Over corresponding bounded ranges, equal probability per unit of the rate gap does not give equal probability per unit of the valuation multiple. An analyst who imposes uniformity in one and another who imposes it in the other have selected different laws over the same possible valuations. They may therefore report different expected values without having observed different evidence. The extra premise is not harmless notation; it is the weighting rule. A symmetry grounded in the problem can sometimes select it, as Jaynes argued. Without such grounds, calling a prior uninformative leaves the choice unexplained.[4]

A related difficulty concerns the similarities through which we learn. Goodman's “grue” and “bleen” demonstrate how rival predicates can agree with all observations made so far while supporting different continuations. Watanabe's treatment of similarity exposes the dependence of classification on the features and weights through which objects are compared. An industry classification, a classification by technological dependence and a classification by exposure to future policy can organise the same firms differently, without changing their recorded histories. Evidence can discriminate among these descriptions, but it need not have done so decisively by the date of an investment decision.[5]

The no-free-lunch results concern a different, complementary issue. Under their comparison assumptions, uniform averaging over the full class of target functions removes a general advantage of one method over another; a useful advantage depends on structure in the problems actually encountered. Their lesson is not that successful learning is an illusion or that all financial models are equally good. It is that demonstrated performance has a domain. Extending a method trained on gradual policy adjustment to a sudden legal prohibition requires a judgment about which useful relationships survive the change. Repeating the computation cannot, by itself, justify that judgment.[5]

The same conditional character appears in the foundations of inference. Cox's derivation works with stated requirements on plausibility; Jaynes's construction requires specified information; Shore and Johnson's consistency arguments include conditions governing how information about separate systems is combined. These results are powerful because their assumptions do work. Treating two sectors as independent is a different premise from treating them as exposed to a common policy shock. Where the observed marginals do not decide their dependence, no amount of exact calculation under independence establishes that independence was the right premise.[6]

Rational agreement inherits such conditions. Stephen Morris opens his examination of the common prior assumption with a sentence worth retaining: “It has become an article of faith among economists that differences in beliefs among rational individuals must be explained by different information.”[7] Aumann's agreement theorem does not establish that article of faith. Within its information model, a common prior and common knowledge of the posterior probabilities of an event force those probabilities to coincide. Rationality alone does not supply the common prior. Blackwell and Dubins, meanwhile, show that predictions can merge as a common record accumulates under an absolute-continuity condition on the laws of complete histories. Mere willingness to assign positive probability to each finite observation is not the same condition, and an asymptotic result supplies no deadline for financially useful agreement.[7]

Persistent disagreement is consequently a reason to investigate, not a certificate of representational underdetermination. Different data, different trust in their sources, mistakes in updating or a thin record may explain it. Once those possibilities have been examined, different defensible resolutions of the residue can still sustain different probability pictures. This is the sense in which all roads lead to disagreement. The conclusion does not confer equal merit on every opinion; it identifies where a substantive commitment continues to influence the answer after the available discipline has been applied.

A committee that resolves a remaining ambiguity is not merely selecting the last setting of a statistical procedure. Its choice can change a project's access to credit, the capital attached to an exposure or whose losses appear in a stress test. When several specifications remain defensible, the institution's mandate must supply reasons for preferring one, and the authority to decide becomes an economic fact of its own. The consequences of choosing still have owners even where the evidence does not choose uniquely.

## What a price can settle

Finance has an observable that appears capable of deciding what the argument has left open: the market price. A quoted price subjects a valuation to an important test and allows participants with very different knowledge to transact. Hayek's account of this coordination remains compelling precisely because it does not require any participant to possess the whole of the relevant knowledge. The achievement becomes a different claim, however, when a price is treated as a transparent statement of what the market believes about the future.[8]

Beliefs enter price formation together with wealth, risk tolerance, endowments, hedging needs, funding conditions and mandates. The same price can therefore arise from different combinations of these determinants. Even a sufficiently rich set of option prices reveals state prices under an extraction model, not an unmediated physical forecast: the value of receiving money in a particular state is not the probability of that state. With only finitely many liquid claims, the prices generally leave a range of compatible valuation pictures. Recovering one comparator requires further restrictions or an explicit selection rule, rather than the assertion that it was already waiting inside “the market”.[8]

This distinction cuts in both directions. Matching current prices is essential when the question concerns a market-consistent value; it does not prove that those prices embody the best forward-looking assessment. Conversely, an internally preferred physical law does not make every difference from market-implied probabilities an error. Risk compensation and the value of state-contingent money cannot simply be removed from the comparison. A claim of mispricing needs a specified financial object and benchmark. Neither the existence of disagreement nor widespread concern about climate pricing establishes a particular discrepancy, still less a profitable trade.

Prices also affect the future against which forecasts will be judged. Cheap finance can prolong a technology, support new investment and enlarge the constituency interested in its survival; restrictive financing can weaken the activity whose prospects the market purports to assess. Merton's self-fulfilling bank run provides the stark case, while MacKenzie's account of financial performativity examines the broader possibility that models help reshape their objects. The implication for long-horizon valuation is that prices must be considered both as evidence and as causes. Later outcomes cannot always be interpreted as though they had been produced independently of the financing decisions based on earlier beliefs.[9]

## When the constraint also binds the critic

Suppose, nevertheless, that a careful comparison leaves a substantial gap between an institution's valuation and the market price. The institution may use it immediately in asset selection, underwriting, financing or hedging, provided a suitable position exists. None of this requires a new market in disagreement. It does require an explanation of why the gap can persist and how this particular investor expects to benefit from it. An explanation of persistence that relies on restrictions on other investors cannot omit the restrictions on the investor proposing the trade.

There is a symmetry here that the aspiring arbitrageur cannot afford to overlook: *A mispricing sustained by a constraint that also binds you cannot be harvested by you.* The relevant meaning of “binds” is that the constraint prevents the position needed to capture the discrepancy, including the ability to carry it until the proposed return is realised. A restriction that merely reduces position size can leave a smaller profitable trade; an alternative security can leave another route open. The claim is about the blocked opportunity, not a general assertion that constrained investors cannot make money. Its force is to require the proposed advantage to survive the very explanation offered for the market's failure to remove it.

Consider an investor who attributes an overvaluation to the inability of pessimistic institutions to sell short. Sharing that inability does not turn a better account of the overvaluation into short-sale profits. Selling an existing holding or underweighting a benchmark may still be valuable, but these actions have different payoffs from establishing a short position. A put option may offer another expression of the view, if it is available, permitted and attractively priced after costs. Its existence cannot simply be assumed, nor can its premium be treated as though it escaped the constraints operating in the underlying market. Each proposed route has to be evaluated on its own terms.

The difficulty is often less explicit than a trading prohibition. A committee can accept that a long-horizon risk is serious while refusing a position whose interim losses it cannot justify; clients can withdraw the capital required to wait for a correction. Shleifer and Vishny's model of limited arbitrage shows how losses and performance-sensitive funding can weaken the investors expected to correct a discrepancy precisely as it widens.[10] A new fund exposing that mechanism may depend on the same redeemable capital and the same short performance record. Its account of why other investors cannot wait will have to survive a discussion with its own.

There is a constructive implication. Where a public rule forces an important class of investors to sell, excludes them from holding an exposure, or directs them towards an imperfect substitute, an investor able to take the other side may be paid for doing so. But the permission to trade is only the beginning of the argument. The constrained flow must matter at the margin, other capital must not absorb it immediately, and the position must have a viable source of return through cash flows, carry, a catalyst or a defensible holding strategy. Financing, liquidity, trading costs and the risks actually borne must then leave something worth earning. What survives may be a constraint premium rather than an arbitrage profit, and identifying the rule is not yet evidence that the premium exists.[10]

This does not reduce investment to exploiting other people's mandates. Better interpretation of shared evidence, better forecasts, different hedges and more effective execution remain possible sources of advantage. It does prevent an epistemic critique from claiming an investment return it has not earned. The reasoning must identify both the financial consequence of the disputed representation and an implementable way of benefiting from that consequence. Where the obstacle is a shortage of knowledge, improving the analysis may help; where it is a binding restriction on action, the proposal must explain what changes in the feasible trade or in the capacity to hold it.

## From private disagreement to a public claim

Much has already been gained before introducing a security. An institution can distinguish a physical assessment from a pricing convention, uncover incompatible assumptions across its decisions, evaluate a premium wedge and choose a better portfolio within its actual constraints. The case for a dedicated disagreement contract starts with a further task: giving a specified comparison a common description and a financial consequence that an independent party can administer. Existing finance already studies how heterogeneous beliefs affect prices and trading. The proposed addition concerns making the declared comparison itself part of the contract.[11]

That requires the parties to state the two probability pictures, identify the financially relevant question, and agree on an observation against which the comparison will be made. The source of the observation, its timing, the treatment of revisions and the settlement rule must be fixed before the outcome is known. A disagreement about the consequences of a transition may then be expressed through a contract on an authenticated default rate or another suitable observation, without requiring a clearing house to certify either participant's private model as true. Admission certifies that a declaration fits the contract; responsibility for its evidential quality remains with the participant.

Some distinctions material to the investor may be invisible to the chosen observation. That unobserved part is the *governed remainder* of the declared disagreement, and it is different from the representational residue. The residue concerns choices left open in formulating the inquiry; the governed remainder concerns what a fixed settlement channel cannot distinguish once the material question and the probability pictures have been specified. A richer authenticated observation can reveal more of the same disagreement, but neither publication nor a contract makes an unobserved distinction disappear.

Nor does the contract obtain its novelty from a cash flow that is intrinsically impossible to replicate. A market complete on the settlement observation can reproduce the payment once the probability pictures and the payment rule have been fixed, subject to the relevant financing and integrability conditions. What the dedicated instrument supplies is the public specification of the comparison, together with its fixing and settlement procedures. Whether that organisation improves financing or creates useful demand has to be established rather than inferred from the formula. Counterparties, collateral and permissible positions remain necessary, and the constraint that blocked an ordinary trade may block the proposed derivative as well.[11]

The second article will examine the payment rule, beginning with relative surprise, and the conditions under which such a comparison acquires a value. Its purpose will be to determine what can be learned and traded from declared disagreement without allowing the contract to claim authority over the rest. The distinction developed here must survive that construction: a public price can make a comparison actionable, but the price is not a verdict on the whole probability picture, and recognising a better account of the future does not dispense with the means of investing in it.
