> “About these matters there is no scientific basis on which to form any calculable probability whatever. We simply do not know.”
>
> — John Maynard Keynes, “The General Theory of Employment” (1937), p. 214.

Modern finance is technically magnificent and epistemically credulous. Its sophistication operates downstream of assumptions that are often treated as settled before valuation begins: which futures count as distinct, what evidence bears on them, which probabilistic model is adequate enough to use, and how much authority current prices should have. When these choices are stable and widely shared, the arrangement works extraordinarily well. Calibration, hedging, optimisation and stress testing can proceed without reopening the construction of the problem at every decision. Because they work so well, the assumptions that make them possible are easy to forget.

For long-dated assets exposed to structural change, however, that working arrangement becomes difficult to defend. Climate transition, infrastructure exposed to contested policy, geopolitical fragmentation and technological obsolescence require commitments today to futures whose relevant features may themselves be disputed. The dispute can concern which mechanisms to represent, which relationships to carry forward and which evidence deserves authority, even when the arithmetic on either side is beyond reproach. This is where my inquiry began: constructing a defensible valuation required an account of the beliefs supporting it, and examining those beliefs required an account of how the problem had been posed. Epistemology arrived as a forced move.

Existing finance names much of this difficulty without quite naming the comparison at its centre. A scenario describes a possible path, a stress test measures an exposure along it, model uncertainty allows competing specifications, and a market price gives the terms on which a claim trades. None, by itself, specifies how two serious accounts of the same financially consequential future should be compared, what could count as evidence between them, or what should follow when the comparison remains unresolved. Throughout this essay, I call such an account a *probability picture*: a belief expressed as a probability law, or through a probabilistic model that supplies one. In the Cox–Jaynes tradition, probabilities represent degrees of plausibility conditional on stated propositions and information, subject to demanding consistency requirements.[1] “Belief” here therefore means something more disciplined than an opinion. Probability theory can discipline beliefs only after we have decided what counts as an event and what counts as evidence.

## Risk, Knightian uncertainty and radical uncertainty

> “There is a fundamental distinction between the reward for taking a known risk and that for assuming a risk whose value itself is not known.”
>
> — Frank H. Knight, *Risk, Uncertainty and Profit* (1921), p. 44.[2]

Standard risk analysis needs the relevant outcomes to be described well enough for probabilities to have definite objects, and enough information about those outcomes for estimation and revision to be meaningful. Historical frequencies are one source of that information; structural modelling, market observations and expert judgment are others. A reasonably stable description allows expected utility, risk-neutral valuation and familiar risk measures to do useful work without requiring anyone to know the true probabilities. Much treasury practice, short-dated option trading and ordinary risk management operates under this working arrangement, in domains where evidence, convention and experience provide enough regularity for action.

Knightian uncertainty concerns a difficulty within an accepted description: we may know the relevant outcomes while lacking a defensible way to assign reliable probabilities to them. Ellsberg's comparison of bets with known and unknown probabilities makes clear why an unchanged set of possible payoffs does not imply an unchanged decision problem. Keynes had in mind questions such as the prospect of war or the rate of interest twenty years hence when he wrote, “We simply do not know.”[2] Finance encounters related difficulties in model risk, regime dependence and uncertain correlations. Caballero and Krishnamurthy give the point an explicitly financial form in their model of a flight-to-quality episode: agents are uncertain about how their own shocks relate to system-wide shocks, and individually conservative responses can leave the economy collectively exposed.[3] Probabilities may therefore be disputed even while the relevant outcomes remain agreed.

Radical uncertainty goes further: even the relevant outcomes, and the way they are organised, may be unsettled. New technologies, institutional reactions or political ruptures can introduce distinctions that an established model never represented. A model of technological improvement may describe the incumbent technology with considerable sophistication while omitting its displacement by another. Enlarging the family of models is then necessary, but the enlargement has to be chosen. Nothing in the old model guarantees that it supplies the right alternatives. Aydogan et al. make a parallel distinction between uncertainty within a model, uncertainty among models, and uncertainty about whether the family contains an adequate model at all; the last of these is close to the problem at issue here.[3]

Scenarios make such questions tractable by reducing an open future to conditional worlds that can be investigated, compared and revised. Their usefulness depends on the restriction they impose. A family drawn too narrowly excludes material possibilities; one drawn so broadly that almost any decision can be defended ceases to discriminate. The boundary of that scenario family is also institutional. Scientific assessments, central banks, regulators, accounting rules and market conventions help determine which observations and distinctions become usable in financial practice. A scenario need not exhaust the future to be useful, but neither its publication nor its inclusion in an official exercise supplies a probability that was absent from its construction.

Climate transition concentrates these difficulties. Physical and engineering constraints coexist with political choices, technological change and unequal consequences across sectors and capital structures. For a long-lived commitment that is difficult to reverse, underestimating transition risk can lock in exposure before repricing, whereas overestimation may take the more revisable form of an opportunity cost. That asymmetry matters for those decisions. Climate science, macroeconomic scenarios and financial evidence all constrain the problem, yet they do not provide repeated observations of the complete transformation.[4] The practical task is to use that knowledge before the commitments have become irreversible, rather than to withhold judgment until the historical record has caught up with the question.

## Bayesian discipline

> “[Probability theory is] the optimal processing of incomplete information.”
>
> — Edwin T. Jaynes, *Probability Theory: The Logic of Science* (2003), p. xiii.[1]

The obvious objection is that models should face the data. They should, and the objection becomes interesting only when we ask which claims the available observations can test. George Frankfurter recalls an editor of a leading finance journal telling him: “We in finance do not care much about philosophy. We care about data and what data can tell us.”[5] The preference overlooks a prior question. Before data can tell us anything about the decision at hand, we must establish what they measure, why observations can be compared and which conclusions the comparison warrants. In familiar settings these judgments can reasonably remain in the background. Under structural change, they become part of the dispute. The editor has not dispensed with an epistemology; he has declined to examine the one he is using.

Historical defaults can constrain a credit mechanism, engineering can rule out an impossible production path, and accounting identities can expose an investment scenario whose resources do not add up. None of this requires a completed climate transition. What it cannot supply is the repeated full experiment needed to validate every long-horizon implication of the model in the usual way. Asking a unique transformation to pass the evidential test of a recurring short-horizon process amounts to asking the world for evidence it cannot supply and mistaking the impossibility for methodological virtue. Backtesting remains valuable wherever history is informative. Historical validation cannot warrant an extrapolation whose structural continuity it has not tested.

A transition path that depends on an infeasible reallocation of labour or investment should fail its diagnostic even if its financial outputs resemble familiar data. A sound accounting identity, by contrast, cannot establish the political likelihood of the policy path built around it. Tests must be matched to the claims they support, rival specifications examined, and rules for revision fixed before the inconvenient observation arrives. This also requires distinguishing an error in a parameter from evidence that the proposed mechanism or model family is inadequate. Adding observations to the wrong exercise will not turn it into the right one.

Bayesian reasoning belongs to this larger practice, although the financial literature often invokes it in a narrower sense. With a prior, a likelihood and a fixed family of hypotheses, Bayes' rule determines how probabilities change when evidence arrives. It can also be performed impeccably within a model that omits the relevant possibility, treats an unsuitable data source as decisive or owes its form to estimation convenience. The broader Bayesian project begins before the prior and continues after the posterior: it examines what information should enter, which families deserve comparison, how their implications should be checked, and how to act when several pictures survive. Robust and ambiguity-sensitive methods contribute to that examination, but they too require reasons for the models and departures they admit.[1,6]

Action may require one operative picture even when inference has not selected one uniquely. An institution can average across a stated family, protect against an adverse member, or complete a partial description by maximum entropy or minimum relative entropy. They solve different decision problems and need not agree. Entropy-based completion is appropriate when the aim is to introduce as little unsupported structure as possible, relative to declared outcomes, constraints and a reference. It can help formulate a prior or complete an assessment after other evidence has been incorporated. Its contribution is a selection rule; the evidential basis remains the stated constraints and reference. Naming the reference and the assumptions about dependence is part of explaining what the principle has selected.[7]

Coherence has an organisational dimension as well. An institution may avoid a Dutch book—a combination of accepted bets that guarantees a loss—while using incompatible transition assumptions in disclosure, investment and risk management. Different purposes can justify different pictures, but departmental separation is not itself a justification. Nor is revising a model in response to evidence the same as changing the rules of revision to accommodate an unwelcome result. The remedy is to state which account governs which decision and whether its grounds can withstand examination over time.[8]

“Since all models are wrong the scientist cannot obtain a ‘correct’ one by excessive elaboration,” wrote Box.[8] More parameters and simulations can examine a representation more thoroughly without establishing its adequacy. Bayesian analysis must use evidence as fully as possible within the proposed models, while also examining the sources of their assumptions: data, structural knowledge, institutional purpose or an unresolved judgment. Broad Bayesian discipline reaches much further than updating, but it is not self-grounding.[8] Its limit is reached when the available evidence and principles still permit more than one consequential representation.

## The representational residue

> “My thesis, paradoxically, and a little provocatively, but nonetheless genuinely, is simply this: probability does not exist.”
>
> — Bruno de Finetti, *Theory of Probability* (1974), Preface.[9]

Why reach for so theatrical a statement here? Because probabilistic language encourages a tempting picture: once a model is coherent, carefully estimated and repeatedly revised, it is easy to speak as though there were a probability already sitting behind the evidence, waiting to be uncovered more accurately. De Finetti's own position is much stronger than anything I need here—he rejected objective probability, not probability theory—but his provocation breaks that picture open. A probability is always a probability *of something*, conditional on some information, expressed in some representation of uncertainty. Those ingredients have to be fixed before the calculus can operate.

The *representational residue* is what remains when logic, the available evidence and the applicable technical principles do not uniquely determine those prior choices. It can concern the states to distinguish, the features that define similarity, the family of explanations to examine or the treatment of dependence. The term applies to what remains genuinely unforced after the relevant discipline has done its work, not to every judgment a modeller makes.[9]

Several independent arguments arrive at this difficulty. Bertrand's chord problem exposes it in the representation of ignorance. Without specifying how a random chord is to be drawn, different apparently natural ways of distributing the choice uniformly produce different probabilities for the same geometrical event. Finance encounters the same issue when an analyst represents ignorance uniformly over a discount-rate gap rather than over the associated valuation multiple. The possible valuations are unchanged, but their probability weights are not. A change of coordinates that preserves the law would change nothing; choosing uniformity anew introduces a different law. Jaynes showed that a symmetry supplied by the problem can sometimes settle the choice. Where no such argument is available, “uninformative” is not an adequate explanation of what the analyst has assumed.[1,10]

The representation of similarity poses a related problem. Goodman's “grue” and “bleen” show how descriptions that agree with the observations already made can imply different continuations; Watanabe shows the dependence of similarity judgments on the features a language makes available.[11] A credit model grouping firms by current industry, another grouping them by technological dependence and a third grouping them by exposure to future policy need not learn the same thing from the same histories. These classifications may each be coherent, and evidence can favour one over another. Until it does, the classification remains one of the premises through which the data acquire predictive meaning.

The second route concerns the problems a method is equipped to solve. The no-free-lunch results deny a general advantage when methods are compared over unrestricted problem classes under the stipulated averaging assumptions.[12] A successful method exploits structure in the problems it actually encounters; its record therefore establishes competence within a domain. Applying a credit model trained under gradual policy adjustment to an abrupt prohibition or technological displacement requires a further structural judgment about which relationships survive—one that its past performance cannot supply, however impressive the record.

The third route concerns the inputs to inference itself. Cox–Jaynes consistency constrains probability assignments once the events or propositions at issue, and the information being conditioned on, have been specified. Entropy-based selection likewise requires evidence constraints, a reference and assumptions about how separate systems compose.[1,7] If two sectors are independent, their information is combined one way; if they share a policy exposure or supply-chain dependence, the same marginal observations can support different joint losses. A rule can be uniquely justified for a stated inferential problem without selecting every feature of that problem for itself.[7]

These arguments do not show that all representations are equally defensible. Evidence can eliminate some, a symmetry can determine a measure, and stronger structural knowledge can settle an apparent ambiguity. Their common implication is that the success of a calculation within its premises does not establish that all those premises were uniquely required. Enlarging the model family can improve the account without automatically settling the reasons for that enlargement. The remaining choice is logical before it is institutional: a committee may have to decide, but its need to decide did not create the underdetermination.

Nor does rationality itself provide the missing common starting point. Stephen Morris writes: “It has become an article of faith among economists that differences in beliefs among rational individuals must be explained by different information.”[13] Aumann's agreement theorem depends on a common prior and common knowledge of the relevant posterior probabilities. Blackwell and Dubins show that forecasts can merge as evidence accumulates when the forecasters' probability laws over complete future histories satisfy an absolute-continuity condition—roughly, when one does not rule out whole histories that the other still treats as possible. Those conclusions inherit their assumptions; rationality alone supplies neither a common starting point nor a timetable for agreement. Continued disagreement calls for an examination of information, the histories each model permits, updating and ordinary error before it is attributed to the residue. Where those explanations have been examined and the evidence still permits different consequential representations, disagreement can remain an outcome of serious inquiry.

The residue also affects what institutions do with the resulting plurality. Comparing two probability pictures requires a purpose: a measure centred on ordinary predictive accuracy and one designed to give exceptional weight to tail events can rank the same disagreements differently. Selecting one picture for action introduces another question. Averaging, protection against adverse alternatives and entropic completion can produce different budgets or capital requirements. The institution's mandate and reasons must determine which procedure it uses. Mathematics can establish what follows from the choice, but where the accepted premises leave it open, authority over the choice is part of the financial problem. It matters whose exposure appears, which risk receives protection and which consequences are left outside the assessment.

## Prices and beliefs

At this point the market price offers an appealing escape from the disagreements of analysts. A quotation is public, transactable and subject to the discipline of other people's capital. Institutions need common prices to coordinate positions, and a model claiming to value current traded claims cannot simply ignore them. Bachelier nevertheless began mathematical finance with conjectures about probability, not a presumption that quotations disclosed the true law.[14] The difference between taking prices seriously and deferring to them is most important when the price is observable but the reasons behind it are not.

Beliefs enter prices alongside wealth, risk bearing, endowments, funding conditions, mandates, hedging needs and horizons. Different combinations can sustain the same quotation. A deep order book establishes that a claim can be traded under prevailing conditions; it does not establish that the participants could express every relevant view, or that the marginal investor's horizon matches that of the institution using the price. Even a sufficiently rich option surface reveals a valuation object—a state-price picture under an extraction method—not a purified physical forecast.[14] State prices reflect both probability assessments and how valuable money is in each state, so they cannot be read directly as physical probabilities. With finitely many liquid claims, the quotations can leave several valuation pictures compatible with them, so recovering one requires further restrictions or a stated selection rule.

This is not a merely hypothetical concern in climate finance. Stroebel and Wurgler's survey of 861 finance academics, practitioners and public-sector respondents found them at least twenty times more likely to say that markets underestimated climate risk than that they overestimated it.[15] The survey establishes widespread disagreement with prevailing pricing; it does not tell us whether respondents were right or why the gap arose. A model that reproduces market prices has established market consistency; the adequacy of the implied assessment for a long-horizon decision is a separate question. A discrepancy between physical probabilities and pricing weights must still be interpreted through the valuation assumptions before it can be called mispricing.

> “The peculiar character of the problem of a rational economic order is determined precisely by the fact that the knowledge of the circumstances of which we must make use never exists in concentrated or integrated form, but solely as the dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge which all the separate individuals possess.”
>
> — Friedrich A. Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945), p. 519.[14]

Hayek's account gives prices their substantial, but different, authority. They allow people with dispersed and incomplete knowledge to adjust their plans without requiring any one of them to understand the whole. That achievement depends on making coordination possible despite differences in knowledge; it does not turn the resulting price into a complete register of knowledge. Grossman and Stiglitz locate a limit within the economics of information itself: in their costly-information model, a price revealing everything would remove the reward that sustains information acquisition.[16] Some knowledge can therefore remain private for reasons internal to market equilibrium.

The reply that a well-founded objection would already have been traded upon assumes that the relevant trade is possible. *A mispricing sustained by a constraint that also binds you cannot be harvested by you.* The relevant constraint blocks the position itself or the capacity to carry it; a size limit may merely scale the opportunity. A pessimistic investor barred from short selling cannot realise short-sale profits, while an alternative instrument matters only if it actually supplies the missing exposure on acceptable terms. Shleifer and Vishny's account of limited arbitrage also shows why performance-sensitive funding may withdraw as the discrepancy widens.[17] An absent corrective trade can therefore reveal a constraint on risk-bearing rather than a resolution of the underlying disagreement.

Prices also help determine the future against which their apparent prescience will be judged. Merton's solvent bank can be destroyed by withdrawals prompted by a rumour of insolvency. Less abruptly, financing conditions can prolong a technology, accelerate its deployment or create a constituency resistant to its replacement; restrictive finance can weaken the activity whose prospects the price purported to assess. MacKenzie's *An Engine, Not a Camera* names this performative possibility.[18] Later evidence bears some imprint of earlier beliefs and valuations.

## Disagreement in financial decisions

The analysis already has financial uses without a new security. An institution may discover that its portfolio is difficult to reconcile with the transition assumptions it publishes, or that two scenario programmes imply incompatible labour, investment and default paths. Such a diagnostic can establish a material discrepancy without assigning it a price. A *premium wedge* requires a further translation: the competing pictures must be used to value the same financial claim. Even then, the prices at which each side would individually be willing to trade—their reservation values—are distinct from the price at which a transaction might actually occur. Identifying the question, comparing its financial consequences and observing a market price remain separate operations.

An investor may also express a view through existing assets, underwriting or hedging. A relative-value position can be based on a well-founded epistemic thesis while remaining exposed to losses and to risks other than the disagreement that motivated it. Arbitrage requires the stronger case of claims whose quoted terms can be combined to guarantee a gain. The usefulness of better reasoning is not confined to that case, just as it is not contingent on creating a market dedicated to disagreement. It can improve ordinary investment and risk decisions while making the grounds of those decisions more explicit.

The distinctive question for a disagreement contract is whether the comparison itself can be specified publicly. A counterparty may declare its own probability picture, or a comparator may be obtained from conventional markets under a stated extraction method. The participant's decision determines what aspect of the difference matters; the settlement observation determines what can be tested. The source, timing and treatment of revisions have to be fixed before the evidence arrives. Where a public observation fails to distinguish an important part of the dispute, that part remains outside the contract. Pricing the observable component leaves the financially relevant disagreement that the settlement observation cannot distinguish with the parties.

Such a contract could support either a position on a view or protection against the outcomes on which that view performs badly. It would add the public specification and administration of the comparison, rather than an intrinsically unreplicable cash flow: sufficiently complete state-contingent markets could reproduce a payment once its terms were known. Whether standardising it attracts genuine demand is a further economic question. In either case, an exchange can decide whether a declaration supports the agreed settlement; it cannot certify the quality of the participant's entire account of the future. A dedicated market does not create responsibility for the underlying representation: the institution already bears it when it chooses a representation for a decision.[19]

## Conclusion

> “The recognition of the insuperable limits to his knowledge ought indeed to teach the student of society a lesson of humility.”
>
> — Friedrich A. Hayek, “The Pretence of Knowledge”, Nobel Memorial Lecture (1974), excerpt.[20]

Finance cannot answer this problem by demanding certainty from a forward-looking model while accepting the assumptions of current practice without examination. Both organise an unsettled future; both owe an account of the evidence and conventions on which they rely. A rigorous inquiry may reject many representations, justify one for a particular use and still leave consequential alternatives unresolved. The disagreement that remains then belongs to the analysis. A price can coordinate a transaction in its presence, but that accomplishment does not relieve an institution of judging the beliefs on which its decisions depend.

That conclusion has practical obligations. The institution must state the picture governing its decisions, explain which constraints support it, distinguish a deliberate selection from an empirical finding, and retain the material alternatives that have not been refuted. It can then identify which consequences are robust, which depend on an unresolved assumption, and what evidence would justify revision. Where the choice determines a valuation, a capital requirement or whose losses enter a stress test, the reasons for it are part of financial accountability. An institution does not escape that responsibility by adopting the conventional picture without comment. Convention is one of the choices under examination.

Climate transition makes the stakes particularly clear, but the argument extends to every consequential commitment whose future the available record cannot uniquely determine. The task is to make the probability pictures explicit, isolate the disagreement that matters, establish what evidence can test it and give it an appropriate financial consequence, while keeping the unresolved part visible. Hayek's closing appeal to humility was a warning against turning limited knowledge into a warrant for far-reaching control. In finance, the same discipline requires the authority claimed for a model, price or contract to remain commensurate with what it can actually establish. Finance can coordinate under uncertainty while saying clearly what its models assume, what its evidence supports, what its contracts can observe, and what remains a matter of judgment. Carveth Read's standard remains apt: “It is better to be vaguely right than exactly wrong.”[20] For a financial system facing risks on a planetary scale, the difference is the difference between coordination disguised as knowledge and knowledge that has earned the right to coordinate.
